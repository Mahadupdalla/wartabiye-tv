UAE, Qatar and Turkey: Is Somalia being used in a rivalry for influence, or are its people benefiting?

A special political analysis of the UAE's, Qatar's and Turkey's relations with Somalia, asking whether Somalia is an arena for foreign rivalry or whether the Somali people truly benefit.

This story was published by Wartabiye TV25.11.2026 · 18:49 (EAT)

Three countries, three agendas – A special political analysis | Wartabiye TV

For a long time Somalia has been in a phase in which foreign countries hold political, economic, security and strategic influence. The collapse of the central government, the rebuilding of national institutions, the country's geographic importance, Somalia's long coastline and its link between the Red Sea, the Gulf of Aden and the Indian Ocean have made Somalia a place many countries keep their eyes on.

Among the countries most visible in Somalia's relations are the United Arab Emirates, Qatar and Turkey. These three countries have varied ties with Somalia that include investment, diplomacy, security support, development projects, economic agreements and political cooperation.

However, there is a question bigger than ordinary diplomatic relations:

Is Somalia benefiting from the competition these countries are waging in the region, or is that competition turning Somalia into a place where foreign powers seek influence and strategic interests?

This question does not oppose Somalia having relations with the world's governments. Somalia needs friends, investment, security support, trade and international cooperation. But every foreign relationship must be built on the national interest, the protection of sovereignty, transparent agreements and benefit for the Somali people.

1. The United Arab Emirates: Investment, ports and political influence

The United Arab Emirates has long been one of the countries most interested in Somalia, especially in trade, ports, security, investment and political relations.

Somalia's importance to the UAE is not limited to relations between the two governments; it is also linked to Somalia's strategic location. Somalia has a very long coastline, important ports and sea lanes used by international trade, which make the country important for regional trade links and strategic waterways.

Projects and agreements involving the ports of Berbera and Bosaso, and the economic ties between different parties, show how Somalia's ports are among the places foreign governments and companies are most interested in.

But every investment in a port, an airport, economic infrastructure or an essential service has two sides: development and strategic influence.

On the development side, investment can create jobs, increase trade, improve economic infrastructure and bring the country new revenue and services.

On the other side, if agreements are not clearly defined, if the benefit to the government and the people is not assured, or if they create political disputes between the federal government and the federal member states, investment can turn into something that harms the country's political unity and the independence of its decision-making.

There is also a serious political dispute that has arisen between Mogadishu and Abu Dhabi. In January 2026 the Federal Government of Somalia announced that it had cancelled agreements it had with the UAE, basing that decision on accusations related to the country's sovereignty and unity. The UAE rejected those accusations.

That dispute shows that relations between Somalia and foreign governments are not limited to trade and investment, but at times have a direct effect on domestic politics, relations between the federal government and the member states, and the direction Somalia takes on regional affairs.

The question the Somali people should ask themselves is this: how much do the major port, trade and infrastructure agreements really contribute to job creation, economic growth and the lives of Somali people?

An investment made in name only cannot be a national success if the people do not feel its results.

2. Qatar: Diplomatic support, political ties and regional rivalry

Qatar has long-standing diplomatic and political relations with Somalia. Doha is one of the regional governments that has ties with Somalia on political affairs, support, cooperation and international relations.

Qatar was also one of the most visible parties in the political rivalry among Gulf countries, especially the dispute between Qatar and some countries in the region, including the UAE.

At various times that rivalry spread beyond the borders of the Gulf, and its effects were felt in countries in the Horn of Africa, including Somalia.

Somalia, located in a strategic place, with a recovering state and domestic politics in which national leaders and federal member state leaders sometimes hold different views, has become a country where foreign relations are strongly visible in domestic politics.

Diplomatic support, economic projects and political relations can benefit Somalia if they strengthen statehood, development and the country's capacity.

However, it is important to ask: is the support Qatar gives Somalia based on a lasting plan to strengthen national institutions and the people's economy, or are there political and strategic interests that are part of the wider regional rivalry?

Every country has its own interests, and it is no surprise that Qatar protects its political, economic and security interests in the region.

But Somalia must make sure that its relationship with Qatar is not limited to political friendship or temporary support, and that it brings tangible things from which the Somali people benefit.

Diplomatic support without visible results in job creation, education, health, economic infrastructure and the development of the country's institutions can become a relationship that greatly benefits governments but is not equally visible to the people.

Somalia needs a relationship with Qatar based on the national interest, mutual accountability and benefit the people can see, rather than a relationship confined to regional political rivalry.

3. Turkey: Strategic friend, security support and economic agreements

Turkey is one of the governments most visible in Somalia, and in recent years relations between Ankara and Mogadishu have developed many dimensions including security, military, education, health, trade, economic infrastructure and diplomacy.

Turkey has implemented various projects in Somalia and plays a visible role in training Somali forces, supporting security institutions, education and health services.

Turkey also has a large military base in Somalia, which is important for training and preparing Somali forces.

Relations between the two countries have reached a level where Somalia and Turkey cooperate on many matters related to security, the economy and strategic interests.

There are Somalis who see Turkey as an important friend that has helped Somalia in many areas of reconstruction and services, especially at times when Somalia faced severe hardship.

But friendship between states is not judged by words and appearances alone. It is also judged by the agreements signed, the benefits both sides receive, the protection of the country's resources and how the people are included in decisions affecting the future.

Agreements on the sea, energy, natural resources and economic cooperation can be major opportunities for Somalia. However, those opportunities require transparency, legality, accountability and the protection of the interests of future Somali generations.

There are also issues concerning Somali citizens living in Turkey, which need to be discussed seriously when there are reports of deportations, immigration laws, citizens' rights and the conditions Somali people are in.

A cited report on the deportation of young Somalis and the situation of other citizens raises questions about the protection of Somali citizens abroad, and the duties of Somalia's government and of the governments it has relations with.

The Somali government must protect its citizens from the problems they may face abroad, using its embassies, diplomatic relations and existing legal channels.

Turkey can be an important friend to Somalia, but every strategic relationship must be built on transparency, the protection of citizens' rights and agreements that clearly set out the benefits for both sides.

4. The rivalry for influence: Is Somalia an arena where foreign countries compete?

Looking at the relations of the UAE, Qatar and Turkey, it is clear that Somalia is not only a country with which these governments have diplomatic relations, but also a place important to their regional interests.

Somalia has:

• A long coastline important for trade and maritime security.

• Ports located on strategic routes.

• A large market and population with business opportunities.

• A geographic position linking the Horn of Africa, the Gulf of Aden and the Indian Ocean.

• Opportunities in fisheries, energy, transport, trade and economic infrastructure.

• Growing political and security importance given the situation in the region.

These factors make Somalia a place many governments want close relations with.

However, the rivalry of foreign governments can become dangerous if Somali politicians use foreign support to strengthen their power at home, or if foreign relations cause disagreement, disputes and political division.

The problem is not that Somalia has many friends.

The problem is when that friendship comes with conditions that are not understood, agreements that are not transparent, hidden political interests or rivalry that it creates inside the country.

Somalia must be a country with relations with many governments, but it must not allow those relations to stand above the constitution, the law, the national interest and the will of the people.

5. What have the Somali people gained?

This is the most important question by which Somalia's foreign relations should be judged.

When a foreign country enters into an agreement with Somalia on a port, investment, security, construction, energy or economic cooperation, it is important to know the direct and indirect benefits the Somali people receive.

Major projects can be judged by:

Job creation: How many jobs that Somalis can do have the agreements created? Are young Somalis getting real job opportunities?

Government revenue: Do the agreements increase the Somali government's revenue? Are there taxes, benefits or revenue clearly presented to the public?

Public services: Do foreign investment and support increase health, education, water, electricity and other essential services?

Economic infrastructure: Do ports, airports, roads and other projects directly or indirectly make life easier for the people and for Somali traders?

Protection of resources: Do agreements on the sea, energy, fisheries and natural resources protect the interests of Somalia and future generations?

Transparency: Can the Somali people get enough information about major agreements, their duration, their terms and the benefit the government receives?

If foreign relations bring major agreements, visible projects and political influence, but the people are still struggling with unemployment, hard living conditions, poor services and limited opportunities, it is right to ask where the benefit of those relations went.

A successful diplomatic relationship is not measured by how many leaders met, how many agreements were signed or how many photos were taken.

Real success is when the Somali people feel a visible change in their lives.

6. Ports, the sea and resources: Who decides Somalia's future?

Somalia has vast natural resources and economic opportunities which, if managed well, could greatly change the country's future.

Somalia's sea, its ports, fisheries, maritime transport, potential energy resources and the country's strategic location are among Somalia's most important assets.

However, those assets need lawful management, transparent agreements, capable institutions and a system that protects the national interest.

If agreements on resources and infrastructure are entered into without giving the people and state institutions enough information, the benefits of those agreements may not be shared fairly.

It is important that Somalia does not rush into every agreement presented in the name of investment or development, but instead evaluates:

• The economic benefit of the agreement.

• The duration of the agreement and its terms.

• Its effect on the country's sovereignty.

• The rights of the government and the people.

• The role of the companies and governments in the agreement.

• How disputes will be resolved.

• How the agreement contributes to domestic economic growth.

Somalia's resources are not property belonging only to the leaders in office today. They belong to the Somali people and future generations.

7. Somali politicians: The national interest or the pursuit of office and power?

The influence of foreign governments can grow stronger when Somalia's domestic politics is built on disputes, mistrust, competition for office and unresolved disagreements.

When Somali politicians disagree on the country's major issues, foreign relations may become a channel specifically used to seek political, financial or diplomatic support.

That can put Somalia in a situation where foreign governments are directly or indirectly present in domestic political rivalry.

It is important that Somali leaders understand that foreign governments do not engage in international relations without interests of their own.

The UAE has its own interests.

Qatar has its own political and economic interests.

Turkey has its own plans and strategies.

In the same way, Somalia must have a clear national plan that stands above its relationship with any single government.

A foreign government should not be seen as a friend who cannot be asked questions.

Not every investment should be seen as one that cannot be evaluated.

Not every form of diplomatic support should mean that Somalia ignores its future interests.

The Somali government must be a friend to the world's countries, but it must not become a government that depends on its friends to reach its political decisions.

8. What position should Somalia take in this rivalry?

Somalia should not have to choose between the UAE, Qatar and Turkey by siding with one and distancing itself from the others.

It is better for it to have a foreign policy based on the national interest, one that allows it to have beneficial relations with different governments while protecting its sovereignty and its decision-making power.

Somalia needs:

1. Transparent agreements: Major agreements affecting resources, ports, security and the economy must be based on law, accountability and transparency.

2. A national foreign policy: Relations with the UAE, Qatar, Turkey and other governments must be based on a national plan that does not depend on changes of politicians or on domestic disputes.

3. Protection of sovereignty: Somalia must have the right to make its own decisions on domestic affairs, security, the economy and international relations.

4. Benefit that reaches the people: Foreign investment and support must be seen in jobs, services, education, health, economic development and a better life.

5. Real accountability: The government and the institutions handling agreements must explain to the people the benefits, costs and results achieved.

6. Diplomatic balance: Somalia must avoid being drawn into the rivalry of other countries, maintaining good relations with different governments while protecting its own interests.

Conclusion: Three countries, one national interest

The UAE, Qatar and Turkey are three governments dealing with Somalia in different ways, each with economic, political, security and strategic interests.

Somalia can benefit from those relations if it manages them well, if it has a national plan, if the agreements are transparent, if the country's resources are protected, and if the Somali people genuinely benefit.

But if foreign relations become a means of backing rival politicians, seeking influence, steering domestic interests or reaching agreements that are not explained to the people, Somalia may once again become an arena where other governments compete, while the people are still waiting for the results of development.

Somalia needs friends, but it needs friends who respect its sovereignty.

It needs investment, but it needs investment that benefits the Somali people.

It needs security support, but it needs security that it manages itself, built on national capacity.

It needs international relations, but it needs relations built on the national interest that do not lead the country into division.

The UAE has its interests. Qatar has the interests it protects. Turkey has strategic plans. But Somalia must have one interest that cannot be negotiated: the interest of the Somali people and the future of the nation.

Real friendship is not measured by how many agreements are signed, how many ports receive investment or how many leaders meet.

It is measured by how much the Somali people gain in jobs, security, justice, services, development and a future they can trust.

Does Somalia need countries that gain influence over its politics, or countries that help it build its own capacity, economy and independence?

That is the question the Somali people, politicians, the government and everyone who cares about the country's future must answer seriously.
